## The Physical & Aerodynamic Challenge of Low Wind Speeds

Generating usable electrical power from low-velocity atmospheric wind is one of the most demanding fluid-mechanical and electromechanical challenges in renewable energy engineering.

### 1. Cubic Power Density Scaling
The instantaneous kinetic power available in an unconstrained wind stream of cross-sectional area $A$ is governed by the classical fluid power equation:

$$P_{\text{wind}} = \frac{1}{2} \rho A V^3$$

Where:
- $\rho \approx 1.225 \text{ kg/m}^3$ (sea-level dry air density, or $\approx 1.151 \text{ kg/m}^3$ under warm inland Indian conditions)
- $A = \pi r^2$ is the swept area ($\text{m}^2$)
- $V$ is the freestream wind velocity ($\text{m/s}$)

Because available power scales with the **cube of velocity ($V^3$)**, the energy density drops precipitously as wind speed decreases:

| Freestream Velocity $V$ | Wind Power Density ($\text{W/m}^2$ at $\rho = 1.151 \text{ kg/m}^3$) | Relative Available Energy |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **$10.0\text{ m/s}$** (Coastal / Commercial wind site) | $575.5\text{ W/m}^2$ | $100.0\%$ |
| **$6.0\text{ m/s}$** (Moderate commercial threshold) | $124.3\text{ W/m}^2$ | $21.6\%$ |
| **$4.0\text{ m/s}$** (Typical inland breeze) | $36.8\text{ W/m}^2$ | $6.4\%$ |
| **$3.0\text{ m/s}$** (Inland average / target cut-in) | $15.5\text{ W/m}^2$ | $2.7\%$ |
| **$2.0\text{ m/s}$** (Gentle breeze / starting threshold) | $4.6\text{ W/m}^2$ | $0.8\%$ |

At $3.0\text{ m/s}$, an engineer has barely **$2.7\%$** of the power available at $10.0\text{ m/s}$ per square meter of swept aperture.

### 2. Aerodynamic Limitations at Low Reynolds Numbers
For small-scale wind turbines with blade chord lengths $c \sim 0.05\text{--}0.10\text{ m}$ operating at $2\text{--}4\text{ m/s}$, the chord Reynolds number:

$$Re = \frac{\rho V_{\text{rel}} c}{\mu} < 100,000$$

In this sub-critical Reynolds regime:
- Thick conventional airfoils (e.g., NACA 0012 or standard 4-digit series) suffer from extensive laminar separation bubbles, early boundary layer separation, and dramatic lift degradation.
- The lift-to-drag ratio ($C_L / C_D$) drops sharply, drastically lowering the aerodynamic driving torque.
- To produce positive starting torque without external motor kick-starting, the blades must incorporate high-camber, thin-profile geometries (such as the Selig S1223) that preserve attached boundary layers and high lift coefficients even at low Reynolds numbers.

---

### 3. The Electromechanical Starting Barrier
A wind turbine can only begin accelerating from standstill if the aerodynamic static torque ($Q_{\text{aero}}$) exceeds the total resisting static torque:

$$Q_{\text{aero}}(V, \theta) > Q_{\text{cogging}} + Q_{\text{bearing friction}} + Q_{\text{gearbox static friction}}$$

Standard commercial small-wind turbines often feature direct-drive permanent magnet generators (PMGs) designed for high rotational speeds. These generators exhibit substantial **magnetic cogging torque** caused by the interaction of rotor permanent magnets with stator iron teeth. Under low-wind conditions ($<5\text{ m/s}$), the small aerodynamic torque generated by stationary blades is unable to overcome this cogging threshold, causing the turbine to remain completely stalled despite the presence of air flow.

## Why Decentralized Low-Wind Harvesting Matters

### The Geographical Reality of Inland Electrification
Utility-scale wind farms are concentrated along coastal corridors, mountain passes, and offshore sites where annual average wind speeds consistently exceed $6.5\text{--}8.0\text{ m/s}$. However, billions of people—including large fractions of rural and semi-urban populations across the Indian subcontinent, Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, and the interior Americas—live in inland plains where average wind speeds range between **$2.0\text{ and } 4.5\text{ m/s}$**.

These communities have historically been excluded from wind energy because conventional small-wind hardware requires wind speeds exceeding $5.0\text{ m/s}$ just to reach cut-in.

### The Intermittency of Solar Photovoltaics
Rooftop solar PV has expanded dramatically, but it suffers from well-known temporal constraints:
1. **Zero nocturnal generation**: Off-grid rural clinics, residential lighting, boundary surveillance, and communication equipment consume steady power from sunset to sunrise.
2. **Monsoon and cloud attenuation**: Heavy cloud cover and particulate pollution can degrade instantaneous PV output by $60\text{--}80\%$.
3. **Diurnal wind complementarity**: In many inland regions, convective ground heating creates steady evening and night breezes, making micro-wind a natural physical complement to daytime solar generation.

## Project Motivation & Personal Origin

The inspiration for **PAWAN SHAKTI** grew out of hands-on observations at the **Visvesvaraya National Institute of Technology (VNIT), Nagpur**. 

Coming from an academic background in **Metallurgical and Materials Engineering**, founder **Koyaneni Yaswanth** recognized that while advanced power electronics and multi-megawatt commercial turbines receive intensive research attention, small-scale micro-wind systems suitable for residential rooftops in low-wind areas remain fundamentally limited by basic mechanical transmission bottlenecks, poor low-Reynolds blade design, and cost.

> *"I wanted to investigate whether a compact mechanical transmission system—specifically coaxial planetary gearing and multi-rotor clustering—could help make small-scale wind generation mechanically feasible and economically practical in lower-wind conditions where standard turbines remain stalled."*

Rather than relying purely on theoretical simulations or commercial off-the-shelf assemblies, Yaswanth initiated a direct experimental program: modeling custom high-lift airfoils in Autodesk Fusion 360, additive manufacturing modular prototypes with integrated 2.5 module gearing, evaluating structural integrity in ANSYS, and validating physical rotation and electrical generation on the test bench and in VNIT's research facilities under the mentorship of Dr. Jawahar Bhukya.
